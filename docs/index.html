DataTasks speech check

Is this recording good enough to pay for?

Drivers and passengers read a sentence into their phone for a few tenge. Before we pay and ship it to a buyer, this check answers: accept, or re-record with a reason.

On 300 recordings it had never seen, this server caught 143 of 150 bad ones (95%) and wrongly rejected 6 of 150 good ones (4%).

What happens to your recording

Cheap checks run on the phone, so a bad take is redone on the spot and never uploaded. The server checks again, since a phone can be tampered with, then listens for the words.

At scale the same function sits behind a queue. Borderline recordings, at most 5% of volume, go to a person instead of an automatic verdict. Accepted recordings are packed into buyer batches that must stay under the buyer's 10% rejection rule.

What the check costs

At the month-12 plan of about 5,000 accepted hours of speech a month (about 1.65 million recordings):

ItemPer monthPer accepted hour
Speech recognition (Whisper large-v3, hosted)$610$0.12
Server, database, storage and delivery (Supabase)$140$0.03
People listening to borderline recordings (5%)$2,750$0.55
Total≈ $3,500≈ $0.70

Listening to everything by hand would cost about $55,000 a month, or $11 per hour, so the check is about 15 times cheaper. It takes about 1.5% of the ~$45 a buyer pays per hour. A native speaker re-listening to 1% of accepted recordings at random, so the buyer gets a measured bad rate per batch, adds about $500 a month ($0.10 per hour).

Why this piece first

Scale lives or dies on quality control: every bad hour that reaches a buyer risks a rejected batch, and every hour checked by hand eats the margin. Spot-checking 5–10% by hand is cheap but lets most bad recordings into the batch and tells the contributor nothing for a day. This check answers in seconds, so the contributor re-records on the spot, and people only listen to borderline cases and the random audit.

Where it still falls short

  • The 95% and 4% come from defects planted in real recordings, so the right answer is known. Real buyer decisions are the next test.
  • Subtle cheating: cutting 20–30% of the recording (about a third of the words) is caught 55 times in 60 (92%), and a person listening to borderline cases raises that to 59. Skipping one to three words was not measured and probably passes. Reading a Kazakh sentence in Russian (40 of 40) and light clipping (60 of 60) were all caught.
  • Kazakh is the weak language: 4 of 49 good Kazakh recordings were rejected (8%), two of them for a pause at the end just over the 4-second limit. Trimming trailing silence before the check is the next fix.
  • FLEURS is read in quiet rooms. A car, a cheap microphone and free speech will be harder. The free conversation task here runs on demo rules that have not been measured yet.
  • Fraud: the same file sent twice is caught. Replaying someone else's recording, text-to-speech and two accounts sharing one voice are not caught yet; they need speaker and synthetic-voice checks. In this demo the page sends the sentence it showed; in the app the server would look it up by task id, so it cannot be swapped.
  • Fairness: if 2% of submissions are bad, about two out of three rejections land on honest contributors. The app needs a one-tap "this is a mistake" that sends the recording to a person, and contributors whose disputes keep winning get their recordings re-checked.

Code and method

The checks, this page, the server function and how the numbers were measured are in the repository.

Targets were ≥90% caught and ≤5% wrongly rejected. With 95% confidence the true rates are 91–98% and 1.9–8.5%, so the 5% target is not proven yet; about 2,000 good recordings would settle it. Text limits were tuned on a separate set from the FLEURS dev split and frozen before this test. Recordings: FLEURS Spanish, Kazakh, Urdu.
